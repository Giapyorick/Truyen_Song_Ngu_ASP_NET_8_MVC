In the past, every late afternoon at twilight, Tràng would return home from work. He walked waddlingly along the skinny path slanting through the market hamlet of the immigrants heading into the wharf area.

He walked while smiling to himself, his small, sleepy eyes gazing into the evening shadows, his heavy jawbones protruding and quivering, making his rough face flicker with thoughts that were both delightful and fierce. He had a habit of talking to himself as he walked, muttering softly about whatever was on his mind.

The neighborhood children, whenever they saw his massive, burly frame coming down the market slope, would swarm out around him, shouting and laughing loudly:
– Ah ah ah… Brother Tràng! Brother Tràng is back, guys!
– Brother Tràng, carry me for a bit…
– Brother Tràng, have you had a drink yet?
– Brother Tràng!…

Some grabbed him from the front, some from behind, others tickled, pulled, or held his legs to stop him from moving. Tràng just tilted his head back and laughed heartily, "hềnh hệch".

That wretched immigrant hamlet would burst into lively commotion for a short while every evening.

But lately, none of the children bothered to run out to greet Tràng anymore. They sat listlessly in dark street corners without moving. In the murky twilight, Tràng walked with weary steps, his faded brown shirt slung over one arm, his shaved, smooth head tilting forward. It seemed as though the anxieties and hardships of a long day pressed heavily upon his broad, bear-like back.

Famine had swept over this hamlet from who knows when. Families from Nam Định and Thái Bình, draped in matting, arrived in crowds carrying and pulling one another along, pale and grayish like ghosts, lying scattered all over the market sheds. People died like falling straw. Not a morning passed without villagers going to the market or fields encountering three or four corpses lying curled up along the roadside.

The air was foul with the damp stench of garbage and the sickening smell of human corpses.

Amidst that gloomy landscape darkened by hunger and thirst, one afternoon the people in the hamlet suddenly saw Tràng return with another woman. His face carried an unusually exhilarated expression. He smirked secretly to himself, his eyes shining brightly. The woman walked about three or four steps behind him. She carried a small basket, her head slightly bowed, her tattered conical hat tilted sideways to cover half her face. She appeared timid and shy. Seeing something unusual, a few children rushed out to watch. Fearing they might tease him as before, Tràng quickly straightened his face, shook his head, and motioned his disapproval.
